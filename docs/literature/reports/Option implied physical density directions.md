# Short-dated options and tail scoring sharpen the evidence

Your result matches what the literature leads you to expect. It does not overturn it. At a one-day horizon, 30-day option-implied information helps a physical (P) density forecast mainly by supplying a better-informed but upward-biased **scale**. Once that scale is recalibrated, the gain shrinks. The closest S&P 500 study found that risk-transformed risk-neutral densities beat historical densities at 2-4 weeks but **lose at one day**, and that blends beat both ([Shackleton, Taylor & Yu 2010](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/45542/1/10.pdf)). So the validation finding is a credible modern replication: +0.033 nats/day from options over historical features, which halves and loses significance after prequential scale recalibration. On its own it is not yet a contribution. The strongest and most novel extensions move the option information closer to the target in one of two ways. The first is **horizon** matching: build a synthetic one-day implied variance or density from the weekly and 0DTE contracts already present in the OptionsDX chains from mid-2012. The second is **location within the distribution**: break the gain into scale, shape and location parts, and localise it in the left tail with censored-likelihood, threshold-weighted CRPS and joint VaR-ES (FZ0) losses. Referees will want asymmetric-GARCH, GARCH-X-VIX and FHS benchmarks, a log-score-weighted blend as the bar to beat, and multiple-comparison control. Because the 2022-2023 test set has already been seen, every new direction has to be built on 2010-2021 data. It then has to be confirmed either on fresh 2024+ data or under a pre-registered, score-once protocol that is labelled honestly. The searches behind this report were limited, and many key papers were checked only at abstract level. The novelty claims below are therefore "no competitor found", not "none exists".

**Verification tags used throughout.** [V] means the paper's existence and the stated finding were checked against an abstract, repository or RePEc record during the research. [V-snippet] means the finding was seen only in a search snippet or third-party summary. [U]/[KB] means the citation comes from background knowledge and its details (pages, volume, numbers) were not re-checked. Check every [U]/[KB] and [V-snippet] item against the full text before it goes into the thesis.

## Options help one-day forecasts mainly through scale, and raw RNDs lose

The established core is narrow but solid. **Implied volatility carries incremental, often dominant, information about future realised volatility at 1-20 day horizons.** For the S&P 100, VIX gave the most accurate out-of-sample forecasts at every horizon tested, and 5-minute returns added little ([Blair, Poon & Taylor 2001](https://core.ac.uk/download/10896152.pdf)) [V]. Implied volatility stays informative after controlling for continuous and jump realised-variance components in a HAR framework, and options appear to anticipate part of future jumps ([Busch, Christensen & Nielsen 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:160:y:2011:i:1:p:48-57)) [V]. But implied variance is biased upward by the **variance risk premium (VRP)**. The VRP itself predicts returns, most strongly at the quarterly horizon and weakly at daily ones ([Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou 2009](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=948309)) [V-snippet; details KB]. Much of that predictability reflects compensation for jump tail risk ([Bollerslev, Todorov & Xu 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:118:y:2015:i:1:p:113-134)) [V]. Splitting VIX² into conditional variance and a premium shows that the two components carry different information ([Bekaert & Hoerova 2014](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18995)) [V].

Turning a full risk-neutral density (RND) into a P density takes a measure change. The literature offers two families of map. **Utility or pricing-kernel maps** have one or two risk-aversion parameters. Calibrating them to realised outcomes shows that implied relative risk aversion **falls with horizon and is lower in high-volatility periods** ([Bliss & Panigirtzoglou 2004](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/option-implied-risk-aversion-estimates-bliss-robert/10005303188)) [V]. A secondary source reports roughly 8 at one week falling to about 2 at six weeks, but that figure was not checked against the original table ([arXiv 1910.14522](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.14522)) [V-snippet]. **Statistical calibration maps** apply a function C to the Q-CDF to get the P-CDF, for example a beta-CDF. These are more flexible and tend to win on likelihood. Closed-form versions of both, fitted by maximising the likelihood of realised index levels, favour a **combination of transformed and historical densities** ([Liu, Shackleton, Taylor & Xu 2007](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/44582/)) [V]. For crude oil, non-parametric calibration significantly beats parametric utility adjustments ([Høg & Tsiaras 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v31y2011i8p727-754.html)) [V]. The deciding S&P 500 evidence comes from Shackleton, Taylor & Yu (2010). Their sample is 1991-2004, their RNDs come from SV and SVJ fits, they use three transformations, they benchmark against ARCH densities from daily and 5-minute returns, and they rank models by out-of-sample likelihood. **Transformed RNDs win at two and four weeks, historical densities win at one day, and mixtures beat either component at short horizons** ([Lancaster eprint](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/45542/1/10.pdf)) [V]. Their effect sizes in nats were not extracted, so do not quote a magnitude until you have read the full tables.

A wider comparison of 15 schemes over more than 21 years finds that RNDs (Variance Gamma best) win on likelihood and information content. GJR-FHS is the most consistent across the density range, and lognormal, Heston and raw Breeden-Litzenberger RNDs are biased and rejected ([Crisóstomo & Couso 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.08007)) [V]. The underlying index is unresolved. One note infers IBEX 35 from the Spanish-regulator working-paper series, and another describes it as S&P 500 1991-2004. Check this before citing it as S&P evidence. On economic value, investors timing the S&P 500 with risk-adjusted one-month implied distributions beat those using historical distributions net of costs, though the advantage weakened in the subprime crisis ([Kostakis, Panigirtzoglou & Skiadopoulos 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:57:y:2011:i:7:p:1231-1249)) [V].

Each of your findings fits a pattern in this literature. The +0.088 nats/day for Q alone over a trailing-RV Student-t is the Blair-Poon-Taylor result restated as a density score: implied scale beats a backward-looking scale. The fact that the Q-over-X gain halves after prequential scale recalibration fits a VRP-biased scale signal and the horizon dependence of the Bliss-Panigirtzoglou risk aversion, since a static mapping from 30-day Q to 1-day P is misspecified. A working paper on S&P 500 leveraged-ETF variance tells the same story for variance forecasts. The raw option signal loses skill, Mincer-Zarnowitz calibration restores it, and the calibrated forecast does not clearly beat HAR, with results depending on the loss ([Goyle & Revtsov, SSRN 7325618](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7325618)) [V-snippet]. The null 2-state HMM result is also expected. Markov-switching models help density forecasts when they use realised variance ([Liu & Maheu, MPRA 71120](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/71120/)) [V]. Markov-switching EGARCH volatility carries information similar to VIX ([MIT thesis](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/153697)) [V]. A recent conditional-kernel study finds that **VIX and the term spread are the most informative conditioning variables** ([Kim, FEDS 2026-059](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026059pap.pdf)) [V]. This suggests the "regime" sits in option-implied state rather than in latent return-based states. Frame your HMM result as encompassing: regime probabilities are spanned by implied scale. Do not frame it as a failure.

## Kernel shape and recovery remain contested, and daily density scoring is largely missing

Two debates surround the measure change, and you need to know both even if you sidestep them. First, the **pricing-kernel puzzle**. Formal tests on 17 years of S&P 500 options find significant monotonicity violations in many months ([Beare & Schmidt 2016](https://www.princeton.edu/~erp/erp%20seminar%20pdfs/papersFall2011/Bearepkpuzzle.pdf)) [V]. Others argue that the violations are an artefact of estimators that ignore conditioning information, and find kernels consistent with monotonicity once that information is used ([Linn, Shive & Shumway 2018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:31:y:2018:i:2:p:493-531.)) [V]. Dalderop and Linton attribute the U-shape to heterogeneous beliefs about conditional volatility ([Cambridge WP](https://www.janeway.econ.cam.ac.uk/working-paper-pdfs/jiwp2405.pdf)) [V]. Kernels with very different economic properties can fit option prices about equally well because statistical power is low ([Heston, Jacobs & Kim, FEDS 2023-053](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2023053pap.pdf)) [V]. That is a direct argument for judging kernels on **physical returns** rather than on option fit. The current frontier estimates the kernel and the conditional return distribution jointly. It finds that losses hurt more in calm markets and that leading macro-finance models miss the conditional distributions ([Schreindorfer & Sichert 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v171y2025ics0304405x2500114x.html)) [V, abstract level only]. Whether that paper reports out-of-sample likelihoods of returns is **unverified**, and it is the main competitor to check before claiming novelty for a tilted-RND extension. A related idea is to tilt a historical density so that its ratio to the RND is monotone, which improves calibration only modestly ([Beare & Dossani 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v18y2018i4p623-635.html)) [V-snippet].

Second, **Ross recovery** leans negative for density forecasting. In theory, Perron-Frobenius recovery identifies true beliefs only when the SDF's martingale component is trivial ([Borovička, Hansen & Scheinkman 2016](https://econbiz.de/Record/misspecified-recovery-borovi%C4%8Dka-jaroslav/10013018669)) [V]. Empirically, recovered S&P 500 distributions are rejected against realised returns and variances by Berkowitz, Knüppel and KS tests, while power-utility-transformed RNDs and historical densities survive the same tests ([Jackwerth & Menner 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:137:y:2020:i:3:p:723-739)) [V]. The positive results concern moment-based market timing ([Audrino, Huitema & Ludwig 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v19y2021i2p291-312..html)) [V] and generalized recovery from many maturities ([Jensen, Lando & Pedersen 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:133:y:2019:i:1:p:154-174)) [V, magnitudes not retrieved]. Neither is a calibrated daily density. **Recovery is a poor use of a few weeks.**

Option-implied bounds on the equity premium also do not help much at daily frequency. The SVIX lower bound averages about 5% a year and passes 20% at the 2008 peak ([Martin 2017](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/67036)) [V]. Chabi-Yo and Loudis bound the conditional premium using risk-neutral variance, skewness and kurtosis, with a mean of about 5.2% ([Chabi-Yo & Loudis 2020](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:137:y:2020:i:3:p:752-786)) [V]. Using these bounds as constraints improves out-of-sample premium forecasts ([IDEAS, Management Science 70(10) 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v70y2024i10p6804-6834.html)) [V-snippet; author-paper match unconfirmed]. A daily premium of a few basis points against roughly 1% daily volatility contributes almost nothing to a one-day log score. Use SVIX² to set a theory-consistent drift if you like, but **daily gains must come from scale and shape**.

What is missing matters most for your positioning. The research found **no 2019-2026 study that scores one-day S&P 500 P densities built from option features by out-of-sample log score with DM tests**. It also found none that uses weekly or 0DTE options for such densities, none that decomposes the option gain into scale, shape and location, and none that learns a Q-to-P map with ML and scores it with proper scoring rules. Recent ML work with option data predicts cross-sectional mean stock returns from IV-surface images ([Kelly, Kuznetsov, Malamud & Xu 2023](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4531181)) [V] or delta-hedged option returns ([Bali, Beckmeyer, Moerke & Weigert 2023](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:36:y:2023:i:9:p:3548-3602.)) [V]. Neural RND work stays on the risk-neutral side ([arXiv 2405.17770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17770v1); [arXiv 1609.07472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.07472)) [V]. Quantile-network return-distribution papers use no option inputs ([arXiv 2408.07497](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.07497v2)) [V]. A functional-PCA regression on the whole option panel reportedly gets monthly out-of-sample R² above 4% ([Chang, Choi, Kim & Park, SSRN 5632350](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=5632350)) [V-snippet], but its target is a monthly mean, not a daily density. These gap claims rest on about 50 queries across the research. A focused SSRN and Google Scholar search for "density forecast" + "option-implied" + "log score" (2021-2026) is a required step before you write the novelty paragraph.

## Matched horizons and the left tail are where stronger evidence lives

The strongest and most consistent pattern across the literature is that **option information matters more when the option maturity matches the forecast horizon**. The 30-day densities in Shackleton-Taylor-Yu lose at one day and win at 2-4 weeks. One-day implied volatility (VIX1D) overstates realised volatility, but a simple VRP adjustment makes it **more precise than HAR and HAR-VIX1D at one day** ([Albers 2025, J. Futures Markets 45(11)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v45y2025i11p2092-2108.html)) [V]. Using VIX1D in VaR reduces the size of losses on violation days ([Springer s41261-025-00306-w](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41261-025-00306-w)) [V-snippet; venue unconfirmed]. VIX1D also carries an overnight bias ([Finance Research Letters 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1544612324002162)) [V-snippet]. Weekly S&P 500 options show **negative jump tail risk that varies independently of volatility** and flag periods of tail concern that volatility misses ([Andersen, Fusari & Todorov 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v72y2017i3p1335-1386.html)) [V]. A separate left-tail factor that volatility does not span also appears in monthly options ([Andersen, Fusari & Todorov 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:117:y:2015:i:3:p:558-584)) [V]. All of this VIX1D work uses the official index from 2023, so it covers only a short sample and is evaluated on volatility or VaR loss. **None of it scores full densities.**

The second pattern is that **option information is worth relatively more deeper in the left tail, but only when combined with historical information**. Adding an implied-volatility quantile regressor to CAViaR raises explanatory power as the quantile moves further left, and the combination matches or beats both pure approaches ([Jeon & Taylor 2013](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/39598/)) [V]. Unadjusted IV-based VaR loses to GJR-GARCH VaR across about 20 years of index data, because of a regime-changing, non-linear VRP that simple corrections only partly fix ([Bams, Blanchard & Lehnert 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v33y2017i4p848-863.html)) [V]. Adding the relative VRP to GARCH greatly improves one-day long-position VaR in developed markets but not in emerging ones ([Slim, Dahmene & Boughrara 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/quaeco/v76y2020icp22-37.html)) [V]. Your Student-t with constant ν cannot express a gain that sits in the tails, so any such gain is currently invisible to the model.

Two other directions are plausible but weaker at one day. Implied skewness corrected for the skewness risk premium predicts future physical skewness better than return-based estimators ([Le et al. 2023](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/91269/7/Le_etal_2023_JFuturesMarkets.pdf)) [V]. However, index skewness *grows* with horizon ([Neuberger 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:25:y:2012:i:11:p:3423-3455)) [V], so one-day skewness gains will be small, and noisy BKM moments in shape parameters risk overfitting. Flexible distributional ML (QRF, NGBoost, MDNs) has no published SPX daily evidence. Its prospects are poor with roughly 3,000-5,000 daily observations and a low signal-to-noise ratio, so it belongs in a robustness appendix at most. Pooling across indices adds fewer independent tail events than the series count suggests, because index crashes are highly correlated.

## Referees will demand asymmetric-GARCH-X benchmarks and tail-aware scoring

Your current benchmark, a trailing-RV Student-t, is too weak to carry a thesis claim on its own. On equity data, **plain GARCH(1,1) is clearly inferior to models with a leverage effect** ([Hansen & Lunde 2005](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:jae:japmet:v:20:y:2005:i:7:p:873-889)) [V]. For VaR, the best performers are heavy-tailed GARCH filters with EVT, followed closely by **filtered historical simulation (FHS)** ([Kuester, Mittnik & Paolella 2006](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jfinec:v:4:y:2006:i:1:p:53-89)) [V]. Because VIX alone already carries most of the forecast-relevant volatility information (Blair-Poon-Taylor), the fair benchmark for any option-based method is **GJR-X with lagged VIX** (or your own SVIX), not plain GARCH. Realised-measure models are the strongest variance benchmarks: HAR-RV ([Corsi 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jfinec:v:7:y:2009:i:2:p:174-196)) [V], Realized GARCH ([Hansen, Huang & Shek 2012](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/10/rp10_13.pdf)) [V] and HEAVY ([Shephard & Sheppard 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:jae:japmet:v:25:y:2010:i:2:p:197-231)) [V]. All three need intraday data. The Oxford-Man Realized Library is believed to have stopped updating in 2022 [U], so check before relying on it. A range-based Parkinson or Garman-Klass HAR is an acceptable proxy if you say clearly that it is noisier. For tails, dynamic semiparametric joint VaR-ES models beat GARCH and rolling-window forecasts on four international indices ([Patton, Ziegel & Chen 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:211:y:2019:i:2:p:388-413)) [V]. Finally, because blends are the robust winner in this literature, the bar is a **log-score-weighted linear pool of the historical and option-based densities**, not either component alone.

On scoring, report the **log score and CRPS** as headline proper scores ([Gneiting & Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)) [KB]. Add tail-focused proper scores. The first is the **censored-likelihood score** ([Diks, Panchenko & van Dijk 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:163:y:2011:i:2:p:215-230)) [V], which its authors illustrated on daily S&P 500 returns. The second is **threshold- or quantile-weighted CRPS** ([Gneiting & Ranjan 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:29:y:2011:i:3:p:411-422)) [V]. Avoid the unrestricted weighted-likelihood score, because it favours forecasts with more mass in the region. For VaR and ES together, use the Fissler-Ziegel family, specifically the FZ0 loss ([Fissler & Ziegel 2016](https://doi.org/10.1214/16-AOS1439)) [KB], with pinball loss for quantiles alone. The Python package `scoringrules` covers CRPS and twCRPS ([docs](https://scoringrules.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)) [V]. Censored likelihood and FZ0 take a few lines each to code by hand.

On inference, use pairwise **Diebold-Mariano with HAC errors** ([DM 1995](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1995.10524599)) [KB] plus the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold small-sample correction [KB], interpreted under **Giacomini-White** ([GW 2006](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecm:emetrp:v:74:y:2006:i:6:p:1545-1578)) [V]. GW is valid for nested models such as X versus X+Q only with a **rolling** estimation window. If your windows expand, either switch or state the caveat. Clark-West ([2007](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:138:y:2007:i:1:p:291-311)) [V] is built for MSE, not log scores, so do not use it for density comparisons. Amisano-Giacomini ([2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500106000000332)) [KB] is the log-score likelihood-ratio form of the same idea. Because you have already run several ablations, referees will expect the **Model Confidence Set**, **Hansen's SPA** and **Romano-Wolf StepM**, all available in `arch.bootstrap` ([arch docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiple-comparison/background.html)) [V]. For calibration, report PIT histograms and Berkowitz tests [KB] ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1198/07350010152596718)). Add the **Knüppel** raw-moment test, which is robust to serial dependence ([Knüppel 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:33:y:2015:i:2:p:270-281)) [V], and **Rossi-Sekhposyan** tests that account for estimation ([UPF WP](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/en/onepaper.php?id=1416)) [V]. For VaR, add Kupiec, Christoffersen and Engle-Manganelli DQ backtests [KB] ([Christoffersen DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527341); [CAViaR DOI](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500104000000370)). For economic value, the **Fleming-Kirby-Ostdiek** volatility-timing fee is the standard ([FKO 2001](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:1:p:329-352)) [V]. Statistical power is the binding limit. Five hundred test days give only about 5-12 exceedances at the 1%-2.5% levels, so tail tests on 2020-2021 alone will be underpowered. Use the full 2012/2013-2021 span with rolling refits for development, and report point estimates with honest confidence intervals.

### Extension 1: a one-day implied measure built from the chain closes the horizon gap

**Hypothesis.** A VRP-adjusted one-day implied variance and short-dated BKM moments (1-7 DTE, interpolated to a one-trading-day horizon) improve one-day P-density log score over both the 30-day features and a GJR-X-VIX benchmark. The gain survives prequential scale recalibration, because it reflects horizon-matched information rather than a level bias. **Method.** For each day from mid-2012, take the two expiries bracketing one trading day, or the nearest 1-7 DTE expiries before daily listings became complete. Extract forwards from put-call parity for each expiry, which is needed because the OptionsDX files do not separate SPX from SPXW and mix AM and PM settlement (project files `data_feasibility_audit.md` and `SPX_data_research.md`). Compute SVIX and BKM moments with your existing integration code ([BKM 2003 PDF](https://people.umass.edu/nkapadia/docs/Bakshi_Kapadia_Madan_2003_RFS.pdf)) [V], interpolate in total variance, and enter the result into the Student-t scale and shape equations alongside the 30-day features. Add weekend and pre-holiday dummies and an FOMC/CPI-day indicator, because 0DTE implied volatility spikes on event days [U]. **Benchmark.** Your X and X+Q30 models, GJR-X-VIX, FHS, and a linear pool. **Evaluation.** Log score, CRPS, censored likelihood below the in-sample 5% quantile, and FZ0 at 2.5% and 5%. Use DM/GW against the strongest benchmark, MCS across all models, and the FKO fee. **Why it is novel.** The VIX1D papers use the official index from 2023 with volatility or VaR losses ([Albers 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v45y2025i11p2092-2108.html)) [V], and Andersen-Fusari-Todorov use weeklies for tail risk and return prediction, not density scoring ([AFT 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v72y2017i3p1335-1386.html)) [V]. A synthetic one-day measure scored on full densities over 2012-2021 has no competitor in the searched literature. **Risks.** Before May 2022, some days lack a one-day expiry, and Tuesday/Thursday SPXW expiries were added only in 2022 ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/how-to-make-the-most-of-added-spxw-options-expirations/)) [V]. Interpolation from 2-7 DTE weakens the "one-day" claim for early years. Wide spreads at very short DTE add noise. The audit suggests weeklies first appear in OptionsDX around June-July 2012, but Cboe listed weeklies earlier [U], so early-sample coverage may be incomplete. That is a data-quality question, not a market fact. Fix the interpolation rule and the event calendar before looking at any scores.

### Extension 2: localising the gain turns "mainly scale" into a measured result

**Hypothesis.** The option gain over historical features comes almost entirely from scale in the centre of the distribution. A smaller but significant residual gain comes from skewness and tail shape and is concentrated in the left tail, where options price crash risk that volatility does not span ([AFT 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:117:y:2015:i:3:p:558-584)) [V]. **Method.** Fit a nested ladder: X; X with option features in scale only; then in Hansen skew-t shape (skewness linear in BKM skewness, ν in BKM kurtosis or a left-tail measure, shrunk toward constants); then in location as well. Also fit an X+Q joint VaR-ES regression in the Patton-Ziegel-Chen style. Report each rung's increment with and without prequential scale recalibration. **Benchmark.** The recalibrated X model, GJR-skew-t, FHS and PZC GAS. **Evaluation.** Score the left tail, centre and right tail separately with censored likelihood ([DPvD 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:163:y:2011:i:2:p:215-230)) [V] and threshold- or quantile-weighted CRPS ([Gneiting & Ranjan 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:29:y:2011:i:3:p:411-422)) [V]. Add FZ0 at 1%, 2.5% and 5%, with DM per region and StepM across the ladder. **Why it is novel.** No paper found splits a daily density-forecast option gain this way. The older comparisons report aggregate likelihood only, while hinting that where a model wins differs across the density range ([Crisóstomo & Couso 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.08007)) [V]. **Why it is low-risk.** It reuses your features and models. Even "it is all scale" becomes a rigorous, citable finding that explains why the Q-over-X gain falls after recalibration. The cost is power: a 0.03 nats/day aggregate gain split into regions is hard to detect on 500 days. That is the reason to develop it on the full 2010-2021 rolling sample.

### Extension 3: modern data can retest the twenty-year-old horizon split

**Hypothesis.** With your 30-day features, the option advantage over historical features grows monotonically from 1 to 5 to 21 trading days, which would reproduce Shackleton-Taylor-Yu on 2010-2021 data. **Method.** Re-run the existing pipeline on h-day log returns, using a Student-t with √h-scaled or directly fitted scale. Score on non-overlapping blocks, or with HAC errors whose bandwidth is at least h−1. **Benchmark.** GJR-t iterated to h days, FHS and the linear pool. **Evaluation.** Log score, CRPS and Knüppel PIT calibration ([Knüppel 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jnlbes:v:33:y:2015:i:2:p:270-281)) [V], which was designed for the serial dependence of multi-step forecasts. **Assessment.** Novelty is modest, since this is a replication with a modern benchmark set and a recalibration control. The result will likely be strong because the direction is predicted by theory and evidence. It is cheap to run. The main risk is that non-overlapping 21-day blocks leave about 120 observations a decade, so the 21-day result will be imprecise. Present this as the context that makes Extension 1's one-day result interpretable.

### Extension 4: tilting the RND toward realised returns is ambitious but contested

**Hypothesis.** A low-dimensional, state-dependent exponential or polynomial tilt of the RND, with parameters linear in SVIX and term slope and fitted by walk-forward maximum likelihood on realised returns, gives a P density that adds to the Student-t regression inside a log-score-weighted pool. **Method.** Extract the RND from the chain, using the shortest maturity available if Extension 1 is built. Map it to one day by total-variance scaling, apply the tilt f_P ∝ f_Q·exp(θ(s)'g(x)), and fit θ prequentially. Alternatively, apply a beta-CDF calibration map, which the literature says tends to beat utility maps on likelihood ([Liu et al. 2007](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/44582/); [Høg & Tsiaras 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v31y2011i8p727-754.html)) [V]. **Benchmark.** Static power-utility transform, static beta calibration, the Student-t regression, and the pool. **Evaluation.** Log score and censored likelihood, DM/GW, Berkowitz and Rossi-Sekhposyan PIT tests ([UPF WP](https://econ-papers.upf.edu/en/onepaper.php?id=1416)) [V], and pool weights over time. **Assessment.** It sits between the static calibration papers and the conditional-kernel frontier ([Kim 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026059pap.pdf); [Heston-Jacobs-Kim 2023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2023053pap.pdf)) [V], and could be novel at the daily horizon. The standalone tilt will probably lose at one day, as Shackleton-Taylor-Yu found. The map from 30 days to one day brings back the scale problem you already have. RND tails depend on extrapolation beyond the quoted strikes. And Schreindorfer & Sichert (2025) may already report out-of-sample return likelihoods. Read their full text before committing; their replication code is public ([Mendeley](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/34n3j4vhkf/2)) [V].

### Reserve items belong in an appendix

A formal encompassing test of HMM regime probabilities against option-implied scale would turn the null into a stated result, with context from Liu-Maheu and Kim (2026). It takes a day, but it has little novelty. Functional-PCA factors of the moneyness × maturity IV surface in the Student-t parameters, with heavy shrinkage, follow the surface-as-input idea of [Kelly et al. 2023](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4531181) [V]. They are feasible, but with about 3,000 observations they are likely to overfit and add little beyond scale. Neural and MDN variants are not advisable at this sample size.

## Fresh data or a score-once protocol must carry the confirmation

The 2022-2023 test period has been observed, and 2020-2021 has already shaped the existing feature set. So a gain found on either window is exploratory unless the protocol below is followed. There is no econometrics-specific standard that prescribes pre-registration for forecasting studies. The following protocol is a synthesis of field norms, namely fixed splits, frozen specifications and SPA/StepM/MCS for data snooping ([Hansen & Lunde 2005](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:jae:japmet:v:20:y:2005:i:7:p:873-889); [arch docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiple-comparison/background.html)) [V]. It is not a cited rule. Develop on 2010-2019 (or 2012/2013-2019 for Extension 1) and tune on 2020-2021. Then commit every specification, interpolation rule, threshold, benchmark, loss and test before any confirmation scoring, which your repository's pre-declare-then-run commit pattern already supports. The clean confirmation is **fresh 2024+ SPX chains**, scored once. OptionsDX coverage and pricing for 2024-2025 is **unverified**, so check it first. If fresh data are unavailable, score 2022-2023 once under the committed specification and label it "pre-registered but not pristine". The analyst knows how those years went (a bear market in 2022), so selection effects cannot be fully ruled out. Report every variant tried, with StepM or MCS across all of them.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what your result means. The +0.033 nats/day is not a weak positive that needs boosting. It is a modern, recalibration-controlled confirmation that 30-day options act at one day mainly as a biased scale signal, which is exactly where the Shackleton-Taylor-Yu horizon split and the VRP literature point. The way to stronger evidence is therefore not more features or flexible learners. It is to measure option information at its own horizon and in its own region of the distribution. A synthetic one-day implied measure from the weekly and 0DTE contracts already in your data, scored on full densities with tail-weighted proper scores against GJR-X-VIX and a linear pool, would address a gap no paper in the searched literature fills. Two results would each be informative. If the gain survives recalibration, horizon mismatch was the bottleneck. If it does not, implied scale is the whole story even at matched horizons. The decomposition and multi-horizon extensions make either result interpretable at low cost.

The main uncertainties are about evidence, not method. Three gap claims rest on absence in a limited search: no daily density scoring of short-dated options, no scale/shape/location decomposition, and no daily-horizon conditional tilt. Several anchor papers were read only at abstract level. Before writing, settle five checks: the full text of Schreindorfer & Sichert (2025), the effect-size tables of Shackleton-Taylor-Yu (2010), the index used in Crisóstomo-Couso, the OptionsDX weekly coverage before 2012 and data availability for 2024+, and every [U]/[KB] citation, including Ross (2015), Jackwerth (2000), Aït-Sahalia-Lo (2000), Rosenberg-Engle (2002), Christensen-Prabhala (1998), Martin-Wagner (2019), Acerbi-Szekely (2014), Patton (2011) and the Fan-Sandri-Taylor HAR density paper. None of these were cited above as evidence because their sources were not confirmed.
